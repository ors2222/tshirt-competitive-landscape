# Product opportunities and next decisions

These are proposals for review, not validated customer needs. The existing Brand Studio work describes creative self-expression as the primary audience hypothesis and children/activity/gifting as a contrasting secondary hypothesis. Neither has been validated with users.

## What to do next, in order

### 1. Establish the actual shirt

Before finalizing a brand promise, identify the blank and obtain its exact manufacturer product page or sample. Record fabric composition, care label, available sizes, measurements, supplier, and any customization constraints. The current loose “relaxed/boxy” description does not establish the garment's actual fit.

**Decision unlocked:** what the product page can safely say about fit, feel, care, and material.

### 2. Test the creative-use idea

If paint or markers are central, run a small, disclosed material test on the exact shirt: record the medium, application, drying, washing, and resulting appearance. Ask the manufacturer for care or compatibility information as a second source. Until this is done, describe paint use as a question under investigation.

**Do not promise:** “paint-ready,” permanent results, machine-wash performance, or included supplies without direct evidence.

### 3. Learn which audience problem matters

Talk with a few people who make or wear customized clothing. Ask about the last time they wanted a plain shirt, how they chose one, what they wanted to do with it, what made them hesitate, and how they judge fit and care. Do not lead with the Blank Theory idea. Keep this exploratory and do not present a small convenience sample as representative.

Compare the creative-wearer hypothesis with the child/activity/gift hypothesis only if there is still a reason to pursue both.

### 4. Compare page concepts with the same facts

Keep garment, price, sizes, facts, and button constant. Try three low-cost page directions:

1. **The Clear Basic:** product-forward photos and verified details, with a clean route to select a size.
2. **The Creative Guide:** the same product information plus an optional, clearly bounded guide to possible customization methods.
3. **The Point-of-View Brand:** a stronger visual expression of the idea, while keeping the product easy to see and the buying facts easy to find.

Ask visitors what they think is being sold, what they would expect to receive, which detail is missing, and what they would do next. This tests comprehension and preference in a small review; it does not prove purchases or market demand.

## Evidence to capture in the portfolio

- What question was being answered and who the page was for.
- The source evidence and uncertainties behind the chosen product facts.
- Which concept was selected and why.
- A small prototype and the exact feedback prompts used.
- What people misunderstood or asked for.
- What changed after feedback—and what remains unknown.

## Claims to hold until verified

| Claim area | What must be checked first |
| --- | --- |
| Fit or “boxy” silhouette | Identify the exact garment and record flat measurements by labeled size; obtain authorized garment images. |
| Fabric, hand-feel, quality | Manufacturer source or physical sample; avoid vague superiority claims. |
| Paint, marker, or wash durability | Test the exact garment and medium using a documented method. |
| Audience demand or satisfaction | Real conversations or usability review; do not invent testimonials or rating counts. |
| Sustainability, ethics, manufacturing | Specific supplier documentation for the exact blank. |

The site should make uncertainty easy to see. That credibility is more valuable than filling gaps with polished but unsupported copy.
